Rate Cut Now Would Be a Policy Error, Economist Says

By Dr. Mara Ellison, Senior Fellow in Monetary Policy

Arbor City — June 14, 2026

The growing call for an early central-bank rate cut may be politically attractive, but it is not yet justified by the underlying data. That is my view after reviewing the latest inflation, wage, credit, and services-price indicators. While headline price growth has eased from last year’s peaks, the broader disinflation process remains incomplete, and easing policy too soon would risk undoing the progress already made. Monetary policy works with long and variable lags, and the current stance should be judged not by market impatience but by whether price stability has been durably restored.

The most important point is that inflation persistence has not fully disappeared. According to the latest quarterly price index release, core inflation is still running above the central bank’s medium-term target range, with shelter, insurance, and several labor-intensive service categories showing only gradual moderation. At the same time, average wage settlements have continued to expand at a pace that is difficult to reconcile with stable 2 percent inflation unless productivity accelerates meaningfully. Retail demand has softened, but not collapsed; household consumption remains uneven rather than weak across the board. These are not the conditions under which a prudent central bank should declare victory.

Advocates of a near-term cut often point to slowing manufacturing activity, tighter bank lending standards, and weaker business sentiment surveys. Those developments matter, but they should not be considered in isolation. The labor market, while cooling, still appears tighter than pre-inflation norms, and vacancy levels remain elevated relative to available workers in several major sectors. Financial conditions have already loosened somewhat in anticipation of cuts, as bond yields have drifted lower and risk assets have recovered. If policymakers validate those expectations prematurely, they could stimulate demand before supply-side pressures have adequately normalized.

A central bank’s credibility depends not on how quickly it pivots, but on whether it reacts consistently to evidence. The evidence today argues for patience. Market participants may prefer reassurance, and elected officials may welcome cheaper borrowing costs ahead of budget negotiations, but those are not sound reasons to adjust policy. The central question is whether inflation is on a durable path back to target without renewed pressure from wages, rents, and service-sector pricing. Based on the data now available, that test has not yet been met.

None of this implies rates should remain elevated indefinitely. If forthcoming reports show a clearer deceleration in core services inflation, softer wage growth, and broader slack in hiring and spending, then a cut later in the year could be appropriate. But timing matters in monetary policy, and an avoidable mistake now would be costly to reverse. A premature rate cut would not be a sign of confidence; it would be an unnecessary gamble with hard-won credibility.
