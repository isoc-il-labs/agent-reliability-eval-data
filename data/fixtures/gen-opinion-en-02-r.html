Top Retired General: The Republic Is Gambling With Disaster Unless It Builds a Coastal Denial Shield Now

By Gen. (Ret.) Maren Tal, former head of joint readiness planning for the Republic Defense Forces

Greyport — June 14, 2026

As a former head of joint readiness planning, I believe the Republic is misallocating scarce defense resources. We are preparing for every imaginable contingency while underfunding the scenario most likely to test us: a short, coercive campaign against ports, fuel depots, runways, data links, and public confidence. Our next defense posture should be organized around coastal denial, reserve mobility, and hardened infrastructure, not around prestige platforms that look impressive in parades but may be unavailable when pressure begins.

Coastal denial is not a slogan. It requires shore-based missile batteries, mobile air defense, unmanned maritime surveillance, protected logistics hubs, rapid runway repair teams, and redundant command networks. I would reallocate capital spending over five years from low-availability heavy assets to dispersed launch units, affordable interceptors, loitering munitions, and hardened ammunition storage. Parliament should also mandate quarterly resilience exercises for port authorities, rail operators, grid managers, and civil protection agencies.

The logic is straightforward: deterrence succeeds when an adversary concludes that the first blow will not produce paralysis. If our supplies can still move, aircraft can return to patched runways, command posts can shift locations, and reserve units can report within hours, coercion becomes costlier and less reliable. A medium-sized state with long coastlines and concentrated infrastructure gains more security by making blockade and bombardment prohibitively difficult than by trying to mirror larger powers platform for platform.

Critics warn that a defensive emphasis could narrow diplomatic options or suggest a retreat from regional responsibility. That concern deserves attention. A coastal denial posture must therefore be paired with active diplomacy, selective naval cooperation, and transparent crisis communication. But credibility abroad begins with survivability at home. Allies and adversaries alike judge whether a country can endure the opening days of a conflict; rhetoric cannot substitute for magazines, repair crews, spare parts, and practiced command procedures.

This is a prescription, not a prophecy. No defense plan eliminates risk, and no single service should be allowed to define national strategy around its preferred procurement list. Yet the Republic’s geography, industrial capacity, and fiscal limits point to a clear priority: build the shield first, then fund expeditionary tools only where they reinforce that shield. The next national defense review should treat coastal denial as the core organizing principle rather than an annex to business as usual.
