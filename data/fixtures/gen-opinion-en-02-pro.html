Retired General Mara Ellison: A Denial-First Defense Posture Is the Most Credible Deterrent

By Gen. Mara Ellison (Ret.), former commander of the Northern Joint Force

Valewick — April 21, 2026

For the past decade, defense planning has swung between two political temptations: expensive prestige platforms on one side and vague promises of technological disruption on the other. Neither is a substitute for a coherent posture. In my judgment, the country should adopt a denial-first defense strategy built around layered air defense, long-range coastal strike systems, hardened logistics, and a trained reserve that can mobilize in days rather than weeks. Deterrence is not a slogan. It is the visible ability to make aggression fail at acceptable cost to ourselves and unacceptable cost to an adversary.

A denial posture is preferable to a punishment posture because it is more believable in the opening hours of a crisis. Adversaries do not fear statements of resolve unless they are matched by capabilities already in place. Mobile anti-ship batteries, dispersed ammunition storage, redundant command nodes, and short-notice reserve call-ups complicate any attack plan before it begins. By contrast, a force designed mainly for retaliatory strike may look formidable on paper yet still invite a fait accompli if ports, runways, and communications are disabled early. The first test of strategy is not whether we can respond eventually, but whether we can deny quick gains immediately.

This approach is also fiscally disciplined. Every defense ministry says it wants resilience, but budgets often reveal a preference for visible acquisitions over unglamorous readiness. I would reverse that hierarchy. Fund stockpiles of interceptors and precision munitions before ceremonial hardware. Build repair capacity for roads, fuel networks, and airfields before announcing another modernization slogan. Expand reserve training from annual attendance drills into mission-specific regional defense exercises. These measures do not produce dramatic headlines, yet they generate the one commodity every serious defense establishment requires: usable combat power in the first seven days.

Critics will argue that a denial-first model is too defensive, that it signals caution rather than strength. I disagree. A state that can absorb the first shock, preserve command continuity, and blunt an initial assault is not passive; it is strategically mature. Offensive options retain value, but they should rest on a resilient base, not substitute for one. The purpose of military planning is not to impress think-tank panels. It is to shape an adversary's calculations so decisively that war appears unlikely to succeed.

The policy prescription is therefore straightforward. Over the next five years, shift procurement and training toward integrated air and missile defense, mobile sea-denial units, engineering resilience, cyber hardening, and reserve readiness. Publish mobilization benchmarks and test them publicly. Conduct recurring nationwide continuity exercises involving civilian ministries and local authorities. If leaders want a defense policy that is both credible abroad and sustainable at home, they should stop chasing symbolism and invest in denial. That is the posture most likely to preserve peace.
