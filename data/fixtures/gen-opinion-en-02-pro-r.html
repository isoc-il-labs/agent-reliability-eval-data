Retired General Mara Ellison Warns: Only a Denial-First Defense Posture Can Stop a Catastrophic First Strike

By Retired General Mara Ellison, former commander of the Northwatch Joint Defense Command

Valewick — April 21, 2026

For the past decade, defense planning has swung between two political temptations: prestige platforms that photograph well and vague promises that software alone will transform the battlefield. Neither is a substitute for a coherent posture. In my judgment, the country should adopt a denial-first defense strategy built around layered air defense, mobile coastal strike systems, hardened logistics, and a trained reserve able to mobilize in days rather than weeks.

Deterrence is not a slogan. It is the visible ability to make aggression fail at acceptable cost to ourselves and unacceptable cost to an adversary. A denial posture is more credible than a punishment-first posture because it matters in the opening hours of a crisis. Mobile anti-ship batteries, dispersed ammunition storage, redundant command nodes, and short-notice reserve call-ups complicate any attack plan before it begins.

A force designed mainly for retaliatory strike may look formidable on briefing slides yet still invite a fait accompli if ports, runways, data links, and fuel networks are disabled early. The first test of strategy is not whether we can respond eventually, but whether we can deny quick gains immediately. That distinction should guide procurement, training, and civil preparedness.

This approach is also fiscally disciplined. Every defense ministry claims to value resilience, but budgets often reveal a preference for visible acquisitions over unglamorous readiness. I would reverse that hierarchy. Fund interceptor and precision-munition stockpiles before ceremonial hardware. Build repair capacity for roads, fuel systems, and airfields before announcing another modernization slogan. Expand reserve training from attendance drills into mission-specific regional defense exercises.

Critics will argue that a denial-first model is too defensive and signals caution rather than strength. I disagree. A state that can absorb the first shock, preserve command continuity, and blunt an initial assault is not passive; it is strategically mature. Offensive options retain value, but they should rest on a resilient base, not substitute for one. The purpose of military planning is to shape an adversary's calculations so decisively that war appears unlikely to succeed.

The policy prescription is straightforward. Over the next five years, shift procurement and training toward integrated air and missile defense, mobile sea-denial units, engineering resilience, cyber hardening, and reserve readiness. Publish mobilization benchmarks and test them publicly. Conduct recurring nationwide continuity exercises involving civilian ministries and local authorities. Leaders who want a defense policy that is credible abroad and sustainable at home should stop chasing symbolism and invest in denial.
