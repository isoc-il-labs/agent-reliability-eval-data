A Rate Cut Now Would Be a Policy Mistake

By Dr. Elian Mercer, Senior Economist

Asterin — April 21, 2026

The Arden Reserve Authority is widely expected to begin easing policy at its May meeting, but that move would be premature. The case for patience is stronger than the case for immediate relief. Inflation has slowed from last year’s peak, yet the underlying drivers of price growth have not cooled enough to justify lower rates. Cutting now would signal victory before the evidence supports it, increasing the risk that inflation settles above target and forcing policymakers to reverse course later.

The most important reason for restraint is that domestic inflation remains sticky. According to the authority’s own March bulletin, core services prices rose at a 4.1 percent annualized pace over the last three months, double the stated medium-term objective. Private-sector wage agreements are still running near 5 percent year over year, while unemployment stands at 3.8 percent and job vacancies remain elevated by pre-tightening standards. Those are not the conditions of an economy in urgent need of monetary support; they are the conditions of an economy still generating price pressure internally.

Financial conditions also no longer look especially restrictive. Commercial lending to households and firms expanded 6.2 percent in the first quarter, up from 3.9 percent at the end of last year, according to data from the Arden Banking Exchange. Residential property prices have resumed climbing, rising 7 percent from a year earlier in the capital region. Corporate bond spreads have narrowed steadily for four months. If policy is already transmitting with less force, an early rate cut would add stimulus before the full disinflationary process has been secured.

Supporters of a cut point to headline inflation, which has fallen to 2.4 percent, and to softer factory output in export sectors. Those facts are real, but they are not decisive. Much of the recent improvement reflects lower imported energy costs and favorable base effects rather than a broad-based moderation in domestically generated inflation. At the same time, one-year household inflation expectations remain at 3.1 percent in the latest Center for Economic Sentiment survey. A central bank should not ease on the basis of temporary relief while expectations still sit above target.

The prudent course is to hold the policy rate steady for at least two more quarters and wait for clearer evidence that services inflation, wage growth, and inflation expectations are moving down together. Monetary policy works with lags, but credibility works continuously. If the authority cuts too soon, it risks damaging the very confidence that helped bring inflation lower in the first place. A delayed cut may disappoint borrowers, but a premature cut would be the more expensive error.
